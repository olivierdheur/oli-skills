# Starter examples: newsletter

Use these only until the user has winners of their own in `content/winners/newsletter/`. They show **how a finished issue is built and how it reads**. Copy the moves, never the words, the creator's facts, or their proof. The user's identity, vocabulary and hard rules still come from `creator-ikigai.md`, `pov.md` and `persona.md`.

Two layers, in this order of weight:
1. **The user's own published issues**, once they exist. Always preferred.
2. **The examples below**: two published issues by Mika Reyes (@its.mikareyes), described by structure.

None of these prove that one structure performs best. They show different ways a personal issue can earn practical value.

---

## Example 1: tactical playbook from a personal result

**"How I Grew 0 → 23K on Instagram with AI (how I'd get started)"** by Mika Reyes (@its.mikareyes)
Link: https://mikareyes.substack.com/p/how-ai-took-me-to-23k-on-instagram

**Progression:** reader demand (friends kept asking) → credibility in the opening (stuck for months, then a specific follower number) → a one-line promise of what this is → **"I cover"** roadmap → why the author creates content → the personal story (setbacks, the advice that changed things) → how they'd start from zero, as numbered steps → the full tool workflow by stage → what didn't help → FAQs.

**Moves worth copying:**
- The opening justifies the post with real demand and a real number in the first few lines, so a cold reader knows why to trust it.
- The roadmap bullets each tease a payoff, not just a topic name.
- The "how I'd start over" section turns the story into steps a reader can run this week, with specific constraints (one platform, one format, a fixed cadence).
- A "what didn't help" section adds honesty and saves the reader time.
- FAQs absorb the objections readers actually raise.

**Leave:** the length (the original runs well past 1,500 words; follow this skill's length), the creator's numbers, tools and personal history.

## Example 2: problem → solution → build guide

**"How I Turned 6 Years of Stale Notes Into an AI Second Brain"** by Mika Reyes (@its.mikareyes)
Link: https://mikareyes.substack.com/p/how-i-turned-6-years-of-stale-notes

**Progression:** a specific personal artifact (a notes database kept for years and never reread) → the shift that made it useful → "you probably have your own version" turn to the reader → the thesis in one bolded line → **"Here's what I share in this post"** roadmap → the problem → the solution and its benefits → how to build yours, as numbered steps with a starter-file list and a ready-to-use prompt → before/after examples from the author's own use → upgrades for advanced readers → a closing analogy.

**Moves worth copying:**
- Opens on a concrete, slightly embarrassing personal object rather than an abstract claim.
- Explicitly tells beginners and advanced readers where to start ("skip to sections 4 and 5 if…").
- Before/after examples show the payoff instead of asserting it.
- Gives the reader something to copy and use (a prompt, a file list), which makes the issue worth saving.

**Leave:** the creator's tools, the sponsor block, and the specific files.

## What to copy vs. what to leave (any example)

**Copy (the writing):**
- A first line that drops the reader into the conversation: a direct question, a quoted line, a scene. No throat-clearing.
- Specific scenes and vignettes over abstract claims. Let the reader feel it before asking them to agree.
- One-line paragraphs for emphasis. Short declaratives. Sentences that sound like a person talking.
- A bold claim immediately backed by a receipt.
- One anchoring analogy that makes an abstract idea easy to retell.
- Honest asides and expectation-setting.
- A fairness beat before a reframe, so the turn reads as earned.
- Conviction: a willingness to take a side.

**Leave (belongs to the original creator, or may break the user's rules):**
- Their catchphrases, interjections, greetings, sign-offs, section titles and jokes.
- Punctuation habits the user's `creator-ikigai.md` bans (em dashes and ellipsis runs are common ones).
- Their audience labels and vocabulary. The user has their own in `persona.md` and `creator-ikigai.md`.
- Their proof and credentials. Use the user's, from the context files.
- Their coined terms and frameworks. Cite them; never present them as the user's.
