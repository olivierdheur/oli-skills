# Starter examples: long-form text posts

Used until the user has winners of their own in `content/winners/long-form-text/`. Once they do, their winners beat everything here.

**Every post below is illustrative.** The creators are fictional, and so are their numbers, clients and stories. Copy the structure and the payload discipline, never the words, and never borrow a number: the user's posts use only facts from their own context files.

## The fictional creators

- **Priya Natarajan**, a fractional CFO who has closed the books for 40+ seed-stage startups. Audience: first-time founders who do their own finance. Platform: LinkedIn.
- **Marco Bell**, a solo developer who grew a scheduling app to $18k MRR without hiring. Audience: indie hackers with a side project. Platform: long X posts.

## Reusable structures

Each structure is a sequence of beats. Pick one in the content-flow step when it fits; don't force it.

- **Numbered Playbook.** Numbers hook → counter-conventional claim → short proof from the user's experience → numbered steps → takeaway → CTA. The default for "how I did X."
- **Story → Framework.** Short personal story → a framework (the user's own, or an outside one with each point mapped to the user's experience) → takeaway → CTA. Keep the story shorter than the framework.
- **Old Way vs New Way.** How it used to be done → what changed → the new playbook, step by step → takeaway. The user narrates; any product is a character, not the subject.
- **Teardown.** The "before" (real, specific) → what was wrong, as a diagnosis the reader can reuse → the "after" → the checklist the diagnosis became.
- **Benchmarks.** A number hook → the full set of real numbers (costs, hours, rates) → what "good" looks like for each → how the reader measures their own.
- **Decision Rule.** A common dilemma → the rule ("if X, do Y; if not, don't") → 2–3 cases showing the rule applied → the rule restated as the takeaway.
- **The Uncomfortable List → Fixes.** A framing line → a list of specific frustrations a narrow group shares → the fix for each. The fixes are the payload; without them it's a vent.
- **Public Reversal → What I'd Do Now.** The take the user used to hold → what proved it wrong → the updated rule or steps.

## Example 1: Numbered Playbook (LinkedIn)

Illustrative. Priya Natarajan. Payload: a checklist.

```text
Your month-end close is a 12-day hostage situation, and the ransom is every decision you can't make until the numbers come back. Mine takes 3 days now, using a five-line checklist.

I've closed the books for 40+ seed-stage startups. The slow ones aren't disorganized. They do the work in the wrong order, so every step waits on the one before it.

Here's the checklist I run on the 1st of every month, in this order:

1. Bank and card feeds. Reconcile every account to the statement balance. Nothing else starts until this matches to the dollar.

2. Revenue. Match every invoice to a payment. Anything unpaid over 30 days gets flagged, not chased yet.

3. The big five expenses. Payroll, rent, cloud, software, contractors. Check each against last month. Anything that moved more than 15% gets a one-line note explaining why.

4. Accruals. Anything you used this month but haven't been billed for. For most seed-stage companies this is contractors and cloud usage.

5. The one-page summary. Cash, burn, runway in months, and the three notes from step 3. That's what goes to the cofounders and the board.

Two rules make it fast:

- Steps 1 and 2 happen on the 1st, no exceptions. Everything downstream depends on them.
- If a number is wrong but small, note it and move on. Fix it next month. A close that waits for perfection never closes.

The founders I work with who run this stop asking me "how much runway do we have?" because they already know, to the week.

TAKEAWAY: Close in dependency order, bank first and summary last, and note small errors instead of stopping for them.

I put the checklist in a one-page template here: [link]
```

Notes: about 290 words. Setup is two short paragraphs; the payload is the checklist plus two rules. A screenshot of steps 1–5 is useful on its own, which is what passes the save test.

## Example 2: Decision Rule (long X post)

Illustrative. Marco Bell. Payload: a decision rule.

```text
I stopped offering a free tier eight months ago. Support tickets dropped 70% and revenue went up 22%. But killing it is wrong for a lot of apps, so here's the rule I use to decide.

Kill the free tier if all three are true:

- Fewer than 3% of free users upgrade within 90 days. Mine was 1.4%.
- Free users send at least as many support tickets as paid ones. Mine sent twice as many.
- Your paid plan costs under $20 a month, so the trial is cheap enough to replace the free tier.

Keep it if any one of these is true:

- Free users invite paid users. Count how many paid signups came from a free user's link. If it's over 10%, the free tier is your marketing budget.
- Your product needs a network to be useful, like shared calendars or team tools.
- You're still finding product-market fit and need the usage data more than the revenue.

What I replaced it with:

- A 14-day trial, no card required.
- An email on day 3 showing the one feature trial users who convert actually use.
- An email on day 12 with the price and a single link to pay.

What happened to the numbers:

- Trial-to-paid: 19%. The old free-to-paid was 1.4%.
- Support tickets per week: from 60 to 18.
- Monthly revenue: up 22% in the first four months.

The mistake I see most is founders keeping a free tier because killing it feels mean. It isn't mean to charge for something that works. It's mean to yourself to support 4,000 people who were never going to pay.

TAKEAWAY: Kill the free tier when free users upgrade under 3%, cost more support than paid users, and don't bring you paid ones.

Bookmark this for the next time you're redoing pricing.
```

Notes: about 310 words. The rule is the payload; the numbers are proof and benchmarks. On X, the first two sentences carry the claim and the proof above the "Show more" cut.

## Example 3: Old Way vs New Way (LinkedIn)

Illustrative. Priya Natarajan. Payload: a workflow.

```text
Bookkeeping for a seed-stage startup used to mean a part-time hire and a two-week lag on every number. For the 11 startups I advise now, it's a bank feed, two rules and a 30-minute Friday review.

The old way:
- A bookkeeper codes every transaction by hand at month end.
- The founder sees numbers two weeks after the month closes.
- Questions go back and forth by email for another week.
- Cost: around $1,200 a month for a company with 300 transactions.

What changed is that the bank feeds and categorization rules in most accounting tools got good enough to do the coding. The human work moved from typing to reviewing.

The new way:

1. Connect every bank and card account to the accounting tool. No exceptions, including the founder's "temporary" personal card.

2. Rule one: every recurring vendor gets an auto-categorization rule the first time it appears. That covers roughly 80% of transactions.

3. Rule two: anything over $1,000 or from a vendor without a rule lands in a review queue instead of being auto-coded.

4. The Friday review. 30 minutes, same time every week. Clear the review queue, check the cash balance against the bank, and write one line on anything surprising.

5. Month end becomes a summary, not a project, because 95% of the work already happened on Fridays.

Cost now: the accounting tool plus 2 hours of my time a month to spot-check, around $400.

The founders who switch don't just save money. They make decisions on numbers from last Friday, not last month.

TAKEAWAY: Let the rules do the coding, and put the human on a weekly review of the exceptions.

The rule list I set up for every new client is here: [link]
```

Notes: about 290 words. The old way is short and quantified; the new way is the payload, in five named steps. The tool is a character, never the subject.

## What doesn't ship

Illustrative. A first draft that fails the save test, and how it was rebuilt.

```text
Three years ago I almost quit consulting. I was working 70-hour weeks, saying yes to everything, and resenting clients I used to love.

Then a mentor asked me one question: "Which of your clients would you pick again today?"

The answer was four out of eleven.

That conversation changed everything. I learned that saying no is a skill, and that the right clients give you energy instead of taking it.

If you're burning out, ask yourself the same question.
```

Save score: 2/5. A story with a moral bolted on. The reader can't do anything with "saying no is a skill."

→ Make it saveable: the payload is hiding inside the story. What made 4 of 11 clients the right ones? Turn that into a 4-question client filter, with the score each question needs, and what the user did with the other 7. The mentor's question becomes the hook's proof; the filter becomes the post.
