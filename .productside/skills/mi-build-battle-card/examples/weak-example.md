# Weak Example: The Card That Got a Rep Corrected

**Synthetic anti-pattern.** Intentionally flawed, and the flaws are hard to spot. It has a thirty-second read, talk tracks, trap questions, a pricing table, and confident positioning. It is well organized and the field liked it. It would pass a quick review — and a prospect corrected a rep from it in week two.

Same fictional Cartelane case, built by a different analyst.

## The Artifact

### Sourcing

Built from a full-spectrum public-source sweep (OSINT, FININT, TECHINT, SIGINT, HUMINT, GEOINT/DEMOINT, MASINT), fused across all seven collection disciplines with confidence stacking applied post-independence-test.

### Executive Summary

Cartelane is a well-funded, fast-moving competitor with strong market momentum and a credible integration roadmap. They win on speed; we win on trust. Our differentiation is clear and defensible, and in competitive evaluations where compliance and reliability are weighted, we are consistently the stronger choice. Reps should position on depth, governance, and enterprise readiness.

### Thirty-Second Read

Cartelane is a venture-backed mid-market competitor with a weak integration story and deteriorating support. We win on reliability, compliance, and depth.

### Say This

1. We have certified SOC 2 Type II compliance; they do not
2. Our integration layer is production-proven; theirs is not shipped
3. We offer full field-level audit lineage
4. Our platform is enterprise-grade and battle-tested
5. Customers report significantly better support experiences with us

### Ask This

1. "How is your support response time these days?"
2. "Do you have SOC 2 Type II?"
3. "How many native connectors do you have?"
4. "What's your uptime been like this year?"

### Watch Out For

- They may claim fast implementation. Our implementation is more thorough.
- They may claim lower cost. Focus the conversation on total value.

### If They Say / You Say

| If they say | You say |
|---|---|
| "We implement in three weeks." | "Speed is easy when you skip the governance layer. Ask what happens at audit time." |
| "We're SOC 2 Type II certified." | "They're not — theirs is still in process." |
| "Our integration platform connects natively to your ERP." | "That's vaporware. Nothing has shipped." |
| "We're cheaper." | "Our total cost of ownership is lower over three years." |
| "We have a large partner ecosystem." | "Most customers use fewer than ten integrations anyway, and each one adds admin overhead." |
| "Our support is highly rated." | "Their response times have degraded significantly this year." |

### Pricing

Growth $119/user/mo, Scale $189/user/mo, Enterprise custom.

### Competitive Positioning

Cartelane competes on speed and price. We compete on trust and depth. In any deal where compliance or reliability matters, we should win.

## Why It Passes a Quick Read

- Structured in the recognizable battle card shape, with all the expected sections.
- The pricing figures are accurate as of the day it was written.
- The SOC 2 and integration points are real, verifiable advantages.
- Talk tracks are punchy and confident — the field will read and use them.
- It gives clear, usable trap questions.
- Nothing in it is fabricated out of nothing.
- It has the call-and-response grid reps ask for, in the right shape, with punchy memorable answers.
- It opens with a summary, which is what everyone asks battle cards for and most cards lack.
- The sourcing line signals rigor: this card is visibly the product of a serious collection process.

## Why It Fails

**There is no evidence appendix and no dates anywhere.** Not one claim traces to a source. *This is the discipline's central violation signal*, and it has two consequences: a rep challenged with "where did you hear that?" has nothing, and the next refresh cannot tell which lines are stale. Six weeks later, the pricing table was still being quoted after another change.

**The summary is a summary, and a seller needed a wedge.** It reads well, it is balanced, and it is the section leadership approved fastest. It is also four sentences a Cartelane rep would be pleased to have written: "well-funded," "strong market momentum," "credible integration roadmap," "they win on speed." Every one is true. Together they reinforce the incumbent's credibility to the one audience that needed a reason to move off it. The rest — "differentiation is clear and defensible," "position on depth, governance, and enterprise readiness" — names three abstractions and not one sentence a rep can say out loud.

*This is the wedge violation signal:* the whole block would sit comfortably on Cartelane's own website, with only the last clause changed. The repair introduces no new evidence, because everything needed is already in the sweep — their partner program closed in July, their entry tier is gone, their floor rose 144% in a quarter. Arranged as a wedge, those same facts say: they are rebuilding their integration layer in-house and the rebuild is landing on customers first. Same truth, and a seller can open a call with it.

**The summary also introduces claims that appear nowhere else.** "Strong market momentum" and "consistently the stronger choice in competitive evaluations" are on no other part of this card, rest on nothing in the sweep, and the second is a win-reason claim with eight-month-old win/loss behind it. This is what happens when the summary is written first: it becomes the entry point for unchecked claims, on the most-read and least-audited surface of the document. The one-pager is a projection of the card, written last, carrying nothing that does not resolve to a line below — and it is the page that gets screenshotted into a deal room, which is exactly why nothing may enter there unexamined.

**The card opens on how it was made.** The sourcing line is accurate — that sweep did run, and all seven disciplines were worked. It is also the first thing a seller reads, and there is nothing in it they can say to a buyer. "Full-spectrum public-source sweep" and "confidence stacking post-independence-test" are how the evidence earned its ratings; that vocabulary belongs in the appendix, where a rep goes to answer a challenge. At the front of the card it spends the reader's attention before the card has said anything, and a skimming reader carries that skim into the section that mattered. *This is the tradecraft violation signal:* discipline acronyms and stage vocabulary in a seller-facing section. Had the same line sat in the header block or beside the appendix, it would be fine — that is where an analyst goes to check provenance. At the top of the card it is the first thing a rep reads. The fix costs one deletion and no evidence.

**The grid is the best-liked section and four of its six rows will not survive contact.** It is the right idea in the right shape — which is why it was adopted verbatim and why nobody audited it.

- Row 1 disputes a true claim. Three-week implementation is corroborated in seven of nineteen reviews. "Speed is easy when you skip the governance layer" asserts something about *their* architecture that no sweep established, and a rep who says it to a buyer with a reference customer loses the room.
- Row 3 says "vaporware" about eleven documented endpoints and a live staging certificate — unreleased, not vapor, and disprovable within two quarters.
- Row 4 asserts our own three-year cost is lower. Nobody modeled it. It is not false; it is unexamined, and it is the row a rep will most want to say.
- Row 6 is the support claim again, now phrased as a reflex a rep will fire without thinking.

Not one response carries an evidence ref, which is the trap-question failure wearing different clothes: a response recited confidently from the card and then corrected costs more than having no response. There are also six rows against a cap of five, and the sixth is the worst of them.

**Every offensive line on this card is the negative image of a Cartelane problem.** Read "say this," the grid, and the positioning section together and count the claims that would still mean something if Cartelane did not exist. There is one — the SOC 2 point — and it is stated as a comparison anyway. Nothing here says what a buyer *gets*: not what their cost looks like at year three, not how fast they reach first production use, not what it is worth that the platform already sits inside systems they own. Row 4 is the section reaching for it and finding nothing, because nothing on our own side was ever collected.

That is not a writing failure and cannot be fixed on this card. The fusion brief behind it carried no own-side rows, and a card collects nothing. A seller holding this document can say what is wrong with Cartelane and cannot say why to buy us.

**There is no Do Not Say section**, and four claims that belong in it are on the card instead:

- **"Deteriorating support"** rests on a public-forum sample, not on contracted SLAs. The prospect who corrected the rep was a happy Cartelane customer with a four-hour SLA.
- **"Customers report significantly better support experiences with us"** is a win-reason claim, and win/loss has been unverified for eight months.
- **"Enterprise-grade and battle-tested"** is unfalsifiable and evidences nothing.
- The read calls their integration story **"weak"** when eleven endpoints are staged and a launch certificate is live — it is unreleased, not weak, and the distinction will be visible within two quarters.

**Two trap questions cannot be evidenced, and one traps our own rep.** "How is your support response time these days?" invites "our contracted SLA is four hours and we meet it," and the rep has nothing. "What's your uptime been like this year?" invites the prospect to compare status pages, which does not favor us. *This is the trap-question violation signal exactly:* questions with no evidence row and no anticipated counter. There are also four questions, where the cap is three — and the fourth is the worst of them.

**"Watch out for" contains no true competitor strength.** "They may claim fast implementation. Our implementation is more thorough" reframes their genuine advantage as a flaw. It is corroborated in seven of nineteen reviews and it is *real*: three weeks against our six, scored in four of four RFPs. A rep told to dismiss it will contest a true claim, lose credibility, and lose the room — which is precisely what happened.

**The thirty-second read never says when we lose.** It lists three things we win on and nothing else. A card that never states the losing condition is fiction, and experienced reps discount the whole document once they notice.

**"Say this" is a feature list with five items.** The cap is four and the language is capabilities, not outcomes. "We offer full field-level audit lineage" describes a feature; "your auditors accept the number without a screenshot trail" describes what the buyer gets. Reps deliver the first as a spec, and buyers hear a spec.

**The pricing table has no capture date and no change history.** Cartelane removed a $49 entry tier this quarter, raising the floor 144%, and widened its annual discount from 10% to 20%. All of that is competitively significant — an entire segment of their base was just repriced and is now addressable — and none of it is here.

**"In any deal where compliance or reliability matters, we should win"** is a conclusion the evidence does not support, stated in a way that gives a rep no handle on the deals where it is not true.

## What Makes This Hard to Catch

It is a *good-feeling* card. Confident, clean, and the field liked it — adoption is the metric battle cards are usually judged on, and this one was adopted immediately. The failure only surfaces in a live call, where nobody from marketing is present, and it comes back as "the customer pushed back and I had nothing," which is easy to attribute to the rep.

The claims are also not fabricated. Every one has a real observation somewhere behind it, stretched by a degree or two: a measured forum sample becomes "deteriorating," a staged capability becomes "weak," an unverified impression becomes "customers report." Each stretch is small. The card is the sum of them.

And the missing sections leave no gaps. A card with no Do Not Say section does not look like a card with something missing. It looks like a card.

## Repair

Build the evidence appendix first and put every claim through it. Whatever fails to earn a row does not go on the card — that step alone moves four current claims into Do Not Say, which is where the run's real value is.

Rewrite the thirty-second read to state when we lose, specifically: speed to first report, three weeks against six, scored in four of four RFPs. Reps will trust the rest of the card because of that sentence.

Cut "say this" to four and rewrite each as an outcome the buyer gets. Cut "ask this" to three, drop the two that cannot be evidenced, and give each survivor its evidence row and its expected counter.

Rewrite the summary as a wedge and write it **last**, after every other section is evidenced: what Cartelane is pushing, and what that push costs this buyer. Then check each of its lines against the card below and delete any with no counterpart — which removes "strong market momentum" and the win-rate claim immediately. What survives is shorter, sharper, and defensible, and it is the page reps will actually carry.

Delete the sourcing line. Keep every capture date and every F/I/A label, and put the tradecraft in the appendix where a rep goes to answer a challenge.

Cut the grid to five rows and give each response an evidence ref, deleting any row that cannot earn one — which removes rows 3, 4 and 6 outright and rewrites row 1 to concede the three weeks before moving the ground to month four. What remains is shorter, and reps will use it, because it survives being said out loud.

Then go back a stage. Ask the fusion brief for own-side rows — our published pricing history, our shipping connector count, our completed certification — and rebuild the offense from claims about us rather than inversions of them. That work happens in fusion, not here; the honest move on this card is to name the gap and say the card cannot yet answer why to buy us.

Rebuild "watch out for" around their genuine strengths — implementation speed first — and concede it plainly, then supply the reframe. Add the pricing capture date and the change history, because the removed entry tier is a campaign, not a footnote. Then date-stamp the card, name the decay by layer, and schedule the weekly SIGINT run that keeps the fastest-rotting half honest.
